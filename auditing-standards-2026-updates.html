Updated Standards on Auditing (SA) — What Changed in 2026

This year's revisions sharpen how auditors assess risk, document judgment and evidence group audits. Here's what changed, and what it means for the businesses being audited.

28 Jun 2026 6 min read J. Karthikeyan & Co. Audit News

The Standards on Auditing don't change often — but when they do, the ripple effects reach far beyond the audit firm's file room. The 2026 revisions tighten expectations around risk assessment, professional scepticism documentation, and group audit oversight. If you're a business preparing for your next statutory audit, here's what's different and what your team should expect.

1. What Changed in the 2026 Revisions

The updates cluster around three areas that auditors — and by extension, auditees — will feel most directly.

Sharper risk assessment requirements

Auditors are now expected to demonstrate a more granular understanding of an entity's IT environment and its impact on financial reporting risk, not just a general control narrative. Where automated systems drive revenue recognition, inventory valuation, or payroll, auditors must document how those systems were evaluated as part of risk identification — not simply relied upon.

Explicit documentation of professional scepticism

Auditors can no longer simply conclude that "no contradictory evidence was found." The revised standards require documented evidence of how alternative explanations were considered and ruled out, particularly around management estimates and related-party transactions.

Stronger group audit oversight

Where a component's financials are audited by another firm or a different office, the principal auditor now carries clearer, more documented responsibility for reviewing that component work — not just for issuing instructions and collecting sign-offs.

The direction of every revision this cycle is the same: less reliance on "we believe management," more requirement to show the evidence trail that led to that belief.

2. Why This Matters If You're Being Audited

These are changes to how auditors must work — but they directly change what your team will be asked for during fieldwork.

  • Expect more detailed questions about your IT systems, especially anything automated that touches financial reporting.
  • Management estimates (provisions, valuations, useful lives) will require more supporting rationale than a single management representation.
  • If your group has subsidiaries or branches audited separately, expect closer coordination requests between the principal auditor and component auditors.

3. Key Points to Remember

  • These changes affect audit methodology, not accounting policy — your financial statements aren't restated because of them.
  • IT general controls documentation is now a bigger part of audit planning discussions — have this ready before fieldwork starts.
  • Management estimates need a documented basis, not just a verbal explanation — build this into your quarter-end close process.
  • Groups with multiple auditors should expect earlier coordination requests, not last-minute component sign-off chasing.
  • Audits may take marginally longer this cycle as auditors adjust to the new documentation depth — plan your close timeline accordingly.

4. How to Prepare Before Fieldwork Begins

  1. Document your IT environment: Prepare a simple narrative of which systems feed financial reporting and what controls exist around them.
  2. Build an estimates file: For every significant estimate (provisions, impairments, useful lives), keep a written basis, not just a number.
  3. Map related-party transactions: Have a current, complete list ready — this is an area of heightened scepticism this cycle.
  4. Align with component auditors early: If subsidiaries are audited separately, coordinate timelines and information requests before the group audit deadline approaches.
Compliance Tip

Don't wait for the auditor to ask for your estimates rationale — prepare a one-page memo for each significant estimate as part of your quarter-end close. It shortens fieldwork and reduces back-and-forth queries considerably.

6. Common Gaps We're Already Seeing

  • No documented rationale for significant estimates beyond a verbal management explanation.
  • IT systems treated as a "black box" with no internal narrative of controls around financial data flows.
  • Related-party transaction registers that are incomplete or updated only at year-end.
  • Group entities coordinating with component auditors only in the final two weeks before the deadline.

Conclusion

None of the 2026 revisions change what your financial statements should say — they change how thoroughly the evidence behind them needs to be documented and reviewed. Businesses that prepare their estimates rationale, IT control narrative and related-party register in advance will barely notice the shift. Those that don't will spend fieldwork answering questions they could have pre-empted.
